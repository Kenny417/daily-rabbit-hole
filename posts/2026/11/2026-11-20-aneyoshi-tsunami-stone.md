# Do Not Build Below This Point: The Tsunami Stones of Japan's Sanriku Coast

In March 2011, a wave came up a narrow, cedar-filled valley in Iwate Prefecture and stopped roughly 300 feet below a four-foot-tall stone. The village of Aneyoshi sat above it. According to a university survey group cited by *The New York Times*, the water had reached 127.6 feet there, higher than the previous Japanese record of 125.3 feet, set by the 1896 tsunami elsewhere in Iwate [1]. The stone carried an instruction to the generations after the people who raised it.

## A coast that keeps being hit

Japan's northeastern shore has hundreds of "tsunami stones". Some are reported to be around 600 years old, though the oldest age is given only as an approximation [2][3]. Most were erected about a century ago, after two deadly tsunamis, including the one in 1896 that killed some 22,000 people [1]. Their inscriptions vary. Many tell the reader to drop everything and climb after a strong earthquake. Others list death tolls or mark mass graves [1][2].

Place names carry the same memory. Nokoriya means "Valley of Survivors". Namiwake, "Wave's Edge", sits about three miles from the sea and marks the farthest reach of a deadly 1611 tsunami [1][2].

## The stone that says where to build

Aneyoshi's marker is unusual. The *Times* reported that it is the only stone that specifically says where houses may go [1]. Its text is usually quoted as "Do not build your homes below this point!", followed by a line about high dwellings and the peace of descendants. Translations of that second line differ between outlets ("ensure the peace and happiness of our descendants", "are the peace and harmony of our descendants") [1][2][3], so the exact wording is best treated as paraphrase until checked against the Japanese original, which I did not consult.

The sequence of events is reported consistently. The 1896 tsunami left two survivors in Aneyoshi, and the village was rebuilt in the same place. The 1933 tsunami left four, and afterwards the village moved uphill for good. The stone was placed after that disaster [1][2]. Residents credit it with sparing the village in 1960 and again in 2011 [1][2].

## What the legend leaves out

The popular version, in which wise ancestors carved a rule and obedient descendants lived, is partly true and partly too tidy.

First, nobody seems to know who made the stone. In 2011 the village leader, Tamishige Kimura, said no current resident knew who erected it, though he described its rule as one that nobody in the village dares break [1].

Second, obeying it did not make people safe from everything. Four Aneyoshi residents died in 2011: a mother and her three young children, swept away in their car in a neighboring town while driving through low ground. The children's grandfather, Isamu Aneishi, told the *Times* the stone "can't save us from everything", and residents said they had relied on it too heavily and had not taught younger people basic survival rules [1].

Third, the stones were widely ignored. The *Times* reported that only a handful of villages kept their homes on high ground, and that many markers were neglected as coastal towns grew after World War II [1]. Some stones were swept away in 2011, which suggests that waves can exceed what even a large marker anticipated [1][2]. The 99% Invisible article adds that some modern readers regard the stones as relics, partly because the archaic language is hard to understand [1][2].

## Memory as infrastructure

What the stones really show is how a community tries to move knowledge across a gap longer than a human lifetime. Large tsunamis on this coast are separated by decades, which is long enough for the people who survived one to die before the next. A stone does not need to be understood by a schoolchild so much as to be seen, and to be placed where building decisions are made. Aneyoshi's version goes further, because it names a rule that can be applied without any knowledge of wave physics.

The 2011 disaster also tested the alternatives. Seawalls helped in some places, but a local amateur historian quoted by the *Times* argued that Japan had relied on them too much and that the waves overwhelmed them [1]. The 99% Invisible piece describes options after the disaster: rebuilding on higher land at higher cost, flood-proofing buildings on existing plots, or rebuilding as before behind higher walls. It also notes that about 74 percent of Japan's land is mountainous, which makes building uphill difficult [2]. Proposals for new warnings range from preserving ruined buildings as memorials to creating monuments suited to television and the internet [1][3]. Whether any of them will outlast the next generations' pull toward the water is not yet known.
