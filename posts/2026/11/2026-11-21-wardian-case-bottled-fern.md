# The Fern in the Bottle: How a Physician's Failed Garden Rearranged the World's Plants

In 1833, two glazed boxes of English plants left London for Sydney. According to the captain's letter, they received a single light sprinkling of water on the way out. On the return voyage, they were not watered at all, and they passed through ice off Cape Horn and tropical heat on the equator. They reached the London docks alive. [1]

The boxes were the work of Nathaniel Bagshaw Ward, a physician who could not keep a fern alive.

## A garden lost to smoke

In his own account, Ward had repeatedly failed to grow ferns because of smoke from nearby factories. His breakthrough, in 1829, was an accident. He had buried a sphinx moth chrysalis in moist soil in a wide-mouthed glass bottle with a lid, to watch it emerge. He noticed that moisture from the soil condensed on the glass and ran back into the mould, keeping it evenly damp. About a week before the moth emerged, a seedling fern and a grass appeared in the soil. [1]

Ward stood the bottle outside a north-facing study window. The plants kept growing for nearly four years with no care from him. The grass flowered once, and the fern produced three or four fronds a year. The experiment ended only when he was away from home, the lid rusted, and too much rainwater got in. [1]

The principle is a small closed water cycle. Plants release moisture into the air, the glass holds it in, and it returns to the soil. Light passes through the glass, so the plants keep photosynthesizing.

## The problem it solved

Moving living plants between continents had long been a near-failure. A Harvard Arnoldia history cites an 1819 report that about one plant in a thousand survived the trip from China to London. [2] When Captain William Bligh made his second attempt to carry breadfruit from Tahiti to Jamaica in 1791, more than two-thirds of the plants were dead on arrival. [3]

Ward tested his idea on the hardest route then available. He sent two cases to Sydney in June 1833 in the care of Captain Charles Mallard. Mallard wrote from Hobart Town on 23 November 1833 that the plants, kept on the poop deck, were "all alive and vigorous." The grasses had grown enough to push at the lids. [1]

The cases were refilled with Australian plants in Sydney in February 1834. Ward reports temperatures falling to 20°F off Cape Horn, with snow a foot deep on deck, and rising to 120°F crossing the line. He says the plants were not watered once and reached London in vigorous condition, with seedlings of one species sprouting en route. [1] (Ward's own timing is slightly off: he says eight months, but February to November is closer to nine.)

## Who gets the credit

The idea was not entirely new. Arnoldia notes earlier glazed boxes, including one from Mauritius and one from Calcutta. [2] The Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh says Allan Alexander Maconochie claimed to have run similar experiments earlier, a claim Ward angrily rejected. Ward's name nonetheless stuck, and he chose not to patent the design. [3] His real contribution may have been publicity: his 1842 book *On the Growth of Plants in Closely Glazed Cases* spread the method, and the nurseryman George Loddiges put more than 500 cases into circulation. [2]

## An engine of empire

Within decades, the box was a standard tool of botanical logistics. The Arnoldia account links it to several large transfers. Robert Fortune moved nearly 20,000 tea plants from China to India, helping found the tea industry of Assam and Sikkim; the article calls this possibly one of the largest acts of botanical espionage. Cinchona, the source of quinine, was moved in secret from South America to Java and India. Brazilian rubber trees went through Kew to Malaya and Ceylon. [2]

RBGE describes a smaller scene that shows how routine the work became: in 1872 a Mr Lister took two cases of plants, including cinchona, to Calcutta, and two plants died. [3] Arnoldia estimates that in about fifteen years William Hooker's Kew imported more plants than in the previous century. [2]

Not every shipment worked, and the story is not a tidy triumph. RBGE points out that some failed 1870s trials in Malawi used moss-packed boxes rather than Wardian cases. [3] Arnoldia says only that the later Washington tea shipment is "suggested" to have achieved little. [2]

## The cost

A sealed box of living plants also carries soil, and whatever lives in it. In 1924, Beverly Galloway, chief of the US Bureau of Plant Industry, wrote that the case had probably been the means of scattering "more dangerous insects, nematodes, and other pests" worldwide. [2] Japanese honeysuckle, first planted in a Long Island nursery row after arriving in such a case, later invaded woodlands across the eastern United States. [2]

Quarantine concerns gradually displaced the case. It was largely phased out by the 1940s, and the last ornamental-plant journeys came in the 1960s. [2][3]

## What it leaves us

The Wardian case shows how a device built for a private reason, to save one man's ferns from London smoke, can become infrastructure. Arnoldia notes that its historical importance has received little specific attention. [2] The property that made it valuable, a sealed world that kept organisms alive across oceans, also made it an efficient carrier of unintended passengers. Its descendant is the terrarium on a windowsill; its legacy is the customs inspection.
