# A Species of One: How Wood's Cycad Came to Survive Only as Copies of a Single Male

Wood's cycad grows in botanical gardens on several continents, including the Temperate House at Kew in London, Durban in South Africa and California in the United States. By one estimate there are around 500 of them [1]. Every one is male, and every one is genetically the same plant. All of them are cuttings, or cuttings of cuttings, taken from a single cycad that grew on a forest slope in what is now KwaZulu-Natal more than 130 years ago [1][2]. No female of *Encephalartos woodii* has ever been recorded [1][2].

The species can't be lost while those gardens keep their plants alive, but it can't reproduce either. Its story shows how a species can end up existing only as copies, and why being copied is not the same as being saved.

## One plant on one slope

In 1895 the botanist John Medley Wood found a large cycad on the edge of the Ngoye (now oNgoye) Forest in Zululand [1][2]. Wikipedia's account, drawing on the botanical literature, describes it as a cluster of four stems from one plant on "a steep south-facing slope" [1]. The Gymnosperm Database describes "a solitary, tall, 4-stemmed plant with a few off-shoots at the base" [2]. Wood searched the surrounding area and found no others [3].

Cycads are an ancient lineage of seed plants. They look a little like palms but are not related to them, and they can take one or two decades to mature [3]. They are also dioecious: each plant is either male or female, carrying either pollen cones or seed cones. A cycad growing alone cannot set seed. The plant on the Ngoye slope was male [1][2].

Wood first classified it as a variety of another cycad, *E. altensteinii*. In 1908 Henry Sander raised it to a species and named it after Wood [1][2].

## Collected out of the wild

The plant's admirers then took it apart over about 20 years. Reconstructions of the dates differ slightly between sources, so the sequence below follows the most detailed one [1] and notes where others agree.

- **1899:** A basal offset was sent to the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew. Kew's own description of its plant says it "was first brought here in 1899" [1][4].
- **1903:** Three more offsets were collected by Wood's assistant Wylie and planted at Durban Botanic Gardens [1]. (His first name is given as James in one source and John in another [1][2].)
- **1907:** Wylie took two of the large trunks to Durban. The Gymnosperm Database says they still grow there [1][2].
- **By 1912:** Only one trunk, about three metres tall, remained in the forest. Wikipedia's account says the largest of the original stems had been "badly mutilated" [1].
- **1916:** The Forestry Department removed that last stem to Pretoria for safekeeping [1][2][3].

After 1916 no wild plant was known. Later searches of the area found nothing [1][2]. Wikipedia reports that the Pretoria trunk is "thought" to have died in 1964 [1]. The species survived because offsets and suckers had already spread from Durban and Kew to other gardens, including Kirstenbosch in Cape Town [1].

Each removal can be seen as a rescue: a lone plant on an unprotected slope was moved into gardens where it would be looked after. The result was still a species that was extinct in the wild within about two decades of its discovery. The IUCN Red List now classifies it as Extinct in the Wild, and international trade in it is restricted under CITES Appendix I [1]. Because a large cycad of a rare species is valuable to collectors, gardens protect their specimens with measures such as alarmed cages [3].

## Why the clones can't help each other

Having hundreds of plants sounds like safety in numbers, but genetically it is one individual repeated. A stand of identical plants shares the same vulnerability to any disease or pest that can kill one of them. In her account of the problem, Laura Cinti of the University of Southampton compares it to the Irish potato famine and the clonal Cavendish banana [3]. And with no female, the clones cannot produce seed, so the species cannot evolve or adapt.

The plants are still alive and growing. Kew's specimen spent nearly a century in the Palm House before moving to the Temperate House in 1997. There, according to Wikipedia's account, it produced its first male cone in September 2004 [1]. The pollen it makes has nowhere to go.

## The backcrossing workaround and its limit

Botanists have tried to breed their way around the problem. *E. woodii* is closely related to the widespread Natal cycad, *E. natalensis*, and the two form fertile hybrids [1][2]. The plan, described in the cycad literature since at least the early 1990s, is to cross a female *E. natalensis* with the male *E. woodii*. Female offspring are then crossed back to *E. woodii* pollen, and the process is repeated for generation after generation [1][2]. Each round raises the share of *woodii* DNA in the offspring. In theory the process ends with a female that is almost entirely Wood's cycad.

"Almost" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Wikipedia's account cites a 2001 study in the journal *Genome* that tested artificial hybrids between the two species. It found that their chloroplasts, the organelles that carry out photosynthesis and have their own small genome, were all inherited from the *E. natalensis* mother [1]. Because chloroplasts pass down the female line, every female in the backcrossing programme inherits them from the original *natalensis* ancestor. However many generations are bred, the descendants will carry *natalensis* chloroplasts. The programme can produce something close to a female Wood's cycad, but by this reasoning never a pure one [1].

With cycads, each of those generations also takes years.

## Looking again

The other option is to find a real female. Since at least 2021, Cinti's project, *Living Dead: On the Trail of a Female*, has worked with Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife, Kew and South African cycad organisations to search the oNgoye Forest from the air [5]. The work has included a light-aircraft flight and drone flights with multispectral cameras, which record wavelengths beyond what the eye can see. One drone mission photographed two plots of about 40 acres each from 80 metres up [5]. By mid-2024, Cinti reported, surveys in 2022 and 2024 had covered about 195 acres of a forest of roughly 10,000 acres, or under 2 per cent [3]. Because no wild Wood's cycad is available to photograph, the team partly trained its image-recognition model on synthetic images [3]. So far, the project reports, "the search continues" [5].

It is also possible that there is no female to find. Some botanists doubt that *E. woodii* was ever a true species. They suggest that Wood's plant may have been an unusual mutant form of *E. natalensis*, a relic of a population that had already died out, or a natural hybrid between *E. natalensis* and another species, *E. ferox* [1][2]. A DNA fingerprinting study in 2006 confirmed that it is closely related to *E. natalensis* [1].

If the plant was a one-off hybrid or a mutant, then the slope at Ngoye held the only one there ever was, and no female has ever existed. If it was the last survivor of a dying population, a female might once have grown somewhere in the forest and may still be there. The hundreds of copies in gardens around the world can't tell botanists which explanation is right.
